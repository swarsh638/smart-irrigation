Introduction

Smart Irrigation Stick waters crops by itself — only when the soil is dry. A sensor box in the field checks how wet the soil is, day and night. When the soil becomes too dry, it sends a wireless message to the pump unit, and the pump starts. When the soil has enough water, the pump stops on its own. The farmer does not need to be there.

Today, many farmers water their fields by guessing. Sometimes the pump runs too long and wastes water and electricity. Sometimes it starts too late and the crop suffers. Many villages also have weak or no mobile internet. That is why our system works without internet, runs on solar power, and lets the farmer see what is happening on a simple phone app called SoilSync, which we are now building.

Theory

1

Measuring soil moisture

We use a capacitive soil-moisture sensor. It sends a tiny electric field into the soil. Wet soil changes this field much more than dry soil does, so the sensor gives a different voltage for wet and dry soil. Its metal parts are covered, so it does not rust in the ground.

The ESP32 reads this voltage and turns it into a percentage. We set 0% by holding the sensor in dry air, and 100% by dipping it in water.

2

Deciding when to water

The system uses two limits. When moisture falls below the lower limit, the pump starts. When it reaches the upper limit, the pump stops. The gap between the two limits stops the pump from switching on and off again and again.

We never water up to 100%, because soil full of water harms the roots. Every crop needs a different amount of water, so we are adding settings for rice, wheat, cotton and soybean.

3

Talking without internet

The two boxes talk to each other using ESP-NOW. It lets two ESP32 boards send short messages directly by radio — no Wi-Fi router, no SIM card and no internet. So it works even in villages with no network.

The SoilSync app, now being built, will connect to a small Wi-Fi hotspot made by the pump unit. This lets the farmer check the field from nearby (about 50–100 m) without internet. Where internet is available, alerts can reach the farmer anywhere through Blynk. Later, a LoRa radio module can carry alerts across the whole village (about 5 km).

4

Switching the pump with a relay

An ESP32 pin is far too weak to run a pump — it gives only 3.3 V and a very small current. So we use a relay: a switch that is turned on and off by electricity. The ESP32 sends a small signal, and the relay connects or cuts the pump's power. The pump's power never reaches the ESP32, which keeps the electronics safe.

The pump is connected so that it stays off by default. If the ESP32 loses power or restarts, the pump stays off, so the field can never flood by mistake. A safety timer in the program will also stop the pump if it runs for too long.

5

Solar and battery power

A small solar panel charges a battery, so the system can work in open fields far from electric lines. The battery gives about 3.7 V, so a small booster raises it to a steady 5 V. This 5 V powers both the ESP32 and the pump, and the relay decides when the pump gets it.

6

From model to real farm

Our model uses a small 3–6 V water pump. A real farm pump (1–5 HP) needs much more power to start. On a real farm, our relay simply controls a bigger switch called a contactor, which runs the pump. The sensor, the boxes and the program stay exactly the same.

Conclusion of theory

Our idea is simple: give water when the soil needs it — not by guessing and not by a fixed timetable. The sensor checks the soil, the program decides, the boxes talk by radio, and the relay runs the pump. The crop gets the right amount of water every time, and the SoilSync app keeps the farmer informed. Because it runs on solar power and needs no internet, it is a low-cost, practical way to save water on farms across rural India.

Objective

We are not just building a machine. We want to make life better for the farmers who feed our country. With Smart Irrigation Stick, we want to:

  1. Help farmers water their crops at the right time and in the right amount, without guessing.
  2. Grow better crops with healthier plants and bigger harvests, by keeping the soil neither too dry nor too wet.
  3. Save water by giving water only when the roots need it — this matters most in dry areas like Vidarbha.
  4. Save electricity and money by running the pump only when needed and cutting the farmer's bills.
  5. Save time and effort so no one has to walk to the field at night or in the hot sun just to switch a pump on or off.
  6. Help every farmer — small, elderly and village farmers too — by keeping it cheap, simple and free from internet.
  7. Protect our soil and nature by stopping over-watering and waterlogging, and by using clean solar energy.
  8. Our dream: a smart village where every farm saves water, every farmer earns more, and science makes rural life better.

Project layout

1. System design

  1. 1Box 1 — Sensor box: a closed box with an ESP32 and a battery. Its moisture sensor goes into the soil. It checks the soil and decides when to water.
  2. 2Box 2 — Pump unit: kept next to the pump. It has a second ESP32 and a relay that turns the pump on and off when Box 1 says so.
  3. 3SoilSync app — the farmer's phone app, now being built and planned for the Play Store and App Store. Near the field, it shows live moisture and pump status through the pump unit's hotspot. With internet, it sends pump alerts anywhere through Blynk.

2. Working flow

Capacitive sensor→Box 1 ESP32→Moisture % vs crop preset
Soil dry→ESP-NOW "PUMP ON"→Box 2 relay→Pump runs
Target reached→ESP-NOW "PUMP OFF"→Pump stops
Every change→Pump unit hotspot (near field) or Blynk (internet)→SoilSync alert
